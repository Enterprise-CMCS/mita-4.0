MITA 4.0 Implementation Walkthrough

This page turns the MITA 4.0 process into a practical walkthrough. It shows what each step is for, what your team does, what you produce, and how the work leads into the State Self-Assessment.

Start the Walkthrough Jump to SS-A Meet the SS-A Tool Back to Overview

Quick Start

The MITA 4.0 process in plain language

MITA 4.0 helps a state move from a business problem to a modernization roadmap. You begin by clarifying the outcome you want, connect that outcome to capabilities, define the architecture needed to support it, and then document the results in the State Self-Assessment.

The basic flow

Problem → Outcomes → Capabilities → Architecture → Assessment → Roadmap

The steps build on one another, so each deliverable becomes input to the next step.

1

Start with the business need

Use Outcomes-Based Planning to define the challenge, the desired result, and how success will be measured.

2

Connect needs to MITA capabilities

Map your outcomes to capability areas and identify current and target maturity.

3

Define how the enterprise should work

Describe the current and future business, information, and technical architecture needed to deliver those outcomes.

4

Document the assessment and act on it

Capture maturity results, brief leadership, set priorities, and turn the work into an executable roadmap.

If you are just getting started

Begin with Step 1

Do not begin with systems or tools. Start by naming the business challenge, the outcome you want, and the metrics that will show progress.

If leadership asks what they will get

Point to the final outputs

The end products include a maturity profile, executive summary, roadmap, and a clearer basis for planning modernization investments.

If the process feels too technical

Use the sidebars on this page

Each step below explains the purpose, who is involved, what the team does, and what comes out of the work in plain language.

Step 1

Perform Outcomes-Based Planning

This step makes sure the work starts with a real business need rather than a technology wish list. The goal is to define what problem the state is trying to solve, what better looks like, and how success will be measured.

At a glance

What this step is for

Turn a business challenge into a clear outcome and a measurable objective.

Who is involved

Program leaders, operational staff, subject matter experts, planners, and anyone responsible for defining goals or measures.

What you produce

A business challenge statement, outcome statements, success metrics, success criteria, and a SMART objective.

What your team does

  1. Name the business challenge in plain language.
  2. Clarify the goals and the concerns blocking progress.
  3. Describe the future outcome the agency wants to achieve.
  4. Choose metrics, time frames, and targets that define success.
  5. Write the result as a SMART objective the team can manage against.
1a–1d

Define the challenge clearly

Identify the business challenge, the goals behind it, the causes of concern, and then summarize that into one clear challenge statement.

  • Describe the current as-is situation.
  • Identify pain points that are real, observable, and changeable.
  • Write one summary statement the team can align around.
1e

Describe the outcome you want

Translate the challenge into a future result the state wants to achieve.

  • Reverse-engineer concerns and drivers by stating what success would look like if they were corrected.
  • Identify the audience that benefits from the change.
  • Identify the capability that must improve and the actor responsible for it.
  • Summarize the result in an outcome statement.
1f

Decide how you will measure progress

Choose the evidence that will show whether the outcome is really being achieved.

  • Identify the success factors that matter most.
  • Define metrics or milestones for each success factor, corrected driver, and expected benefit.
1g–1h

Set the target and write the objective

Define the time frame, target measurements, and success criteria, then write one SMART objective.

  • Determine when the goal should be achieved.
  • Set target measures that indicate success.
  • Summarize the result as a Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound objective.

What good looks like

By the end of Step 1, the team should be able to explain the business challenge, the desired future state, and the evidence that will show whether the state is making progress.

Step 2

Identify Capabilities

This step connects business outcomes to the MITA 4.0 capability structure. It helps the state identify which capabilities matter most and what maturity level is needed to support the target outcome.

At a glance

What this step is for

Link business outcomes to MITA capability areas and define current versus target maturity.

Who is involved

Program staff, planning leads, self-assessment teams, and anyone mapping outcomes to enterprise capabilities.

What you produce

A capability mapping and a starting view of current and target maturity for the capabilities tied to your outcomes.

What your team does

  1. Map outcomes from Step 1 to the MITA 4.0 capability areas.
  2. Bring in any state-specific capabilities that already exist.
  3. Document current maturity where assessments already exist.
  4. Set target maturity for the capabilities that support the desired outcomes.
  5. Refine the mapping as the work becomes more detailed.
2a

Map state-specific capabilities

If the state already has its own capabilities defined, map them to MITA 4.0 capability areas.

2b

Map outcomes to capability areas

Connect each outcome from Step 1 to the capability areas needed to achieve it. More than one outcome may map to the same capability area.

2c

Document current maturity

Capture the latest available maturity assessment for the capabilities related to those outcomes.

2d

Set target maturity

Define the maturity level the state wants to reach for each capability area tied to the outcome.

2e

Update as the work evolves

Refine the capability mapping as state-specific capabilities are clarified and additional maturity work is completed.

Why this matters

This step creates the bridge between business priorities and architecture work. Without it, the self-assessment can become a disconnected inventory instead of a modernization planning tool.

Step 3

Define Architecture

This step describes how the enterprise works today, how it should work in the future, and what gaps must be closed to support the desired capabilities and maturity levels.

At a glance

What this step is for

Define baseline and target business, information, and technical architecture for the capabilities you want to improve.

Who is involved

Enterprise architects, business architects, information and data leads, technical architects, security leads, and subject matter experts.

What you produce

Architecture views, capability-aligned models, a ConOps, and a gap analysis that supports planning and roadmapping.

What your team does

  1. Choose the viewpoints, reference models, requirements, and tools the state will use.
  2. Describe the current baseline architecture across business, information, and technology.
  3. Describe the target architecture needed to achieve the desired outcomes.
  4. Align the three architecture perspectives and develop a ConOps.
  5. Compare baseline and target state to identify gaps, reuse opportunities, risks, and requirements.
3.1

Choose the architectural approach

Before modeling anything, decide which viewpoints, reference models, requirements, and tools the team will use.

Business Architecture

  • Select viewpoints, modeling approach, catalogs, matrices, and diagrams.
  • Identify business requirements to collect.
  • Select business capability and business process reference models.
  • Select the tools needed to create the artifacts.

Information Architecture

  • Select viewpoints, modeling approach, catalogs, matrices, and diagrams.
  • Identify information and data requirements to collect.
  • Select the data reference models that apply.
  • Select the tools needed to document the views.

Technical Architecture

  • Identify the technical viewpoints to use.
  • Identify functional, non-functional, regulatory, compliance, integration, and technology-constraint requirements.
  • Select the required technical reference model.
  • Select the tools needed for technical documentation.
3.2

Describe the baseline capability architecture

Document how the enterprise works today across the three architecture perspectives.

Baseline Business Architecture

  • Identify how processes are currently performed, including measures, metrics, and reports.
  • Identify current activities, roles, stakeholders, standards, and regulations.
  • Gather supporting documentation such as SOPs and desk manuals.
  • Assess current performance and baseline business architecture maturity.

Baseline Information Architecture

  • Identify how data is currently used, including measures, metrics, and reports.
  • Identify data assets, data governance stakeholders, and adopted standards.
  • Gather supporting documentation such as data dictionaries.
  • Assess current data quality and baseline information architecture maturity.

Baseline Technical Architecture

  • Create an inventory of existing applications and technical components.
  • Identify technical roles, standards, service levels, constraints, risks, and technical debt.
  • Map components to business processes, capabilities, and data assets.
  • Assess baseline technical architecture maturity.
3.3

Describe the target capability architecture

Document the future state needed to support the desired outcomes and target maturity.

Target Business Architecture

  • Assess target business capability maturity.
  • Define target processes, activities, measures, reports, roles, and stakeholders.
  • Identify required standards and regulations.
  • Develop target business architecture artifacts.

Target Information Architecture

  • Assess target information architecture maturity.
  • Define target data usage, data sources, relationships, governance roles, and standards.
  • Define key data elements, data quality rules, and metrics.
  • Develop target information architecture views.

Target Technical Architecture

  • Define the target technical architecture maturity level.
  • Set the technology strategy, integration patterns, hosting model, security, and compliance approach.
  • Rationalize the application portfolio and define the future components.
  • Set target service levels, non-functional requirements, and technical debt remediation plans.
3.4

Align architectures and write the ConOps

Connect business processes, data, applications, technologies, and integration patterns so the target enterprise operates as one coherent model.

  • Align information architecture to business processes and policies.
  • Map applications to business capabilities and processes.
  • Map technology components to technical capabilities.
  • Define integration architecture and write the Concept of Operations.
3.5

Perform gap analysis

Compare baseline and target state to identify what needs to change and what can be reused.

  • Compare activities, roles, metrics, and standards across current and target state.
  • Identify gaps, reuse opportunities, and maturity differences.
  • Develop the requirements needed to close the gaps.

What good looks like

By the end of Step 3, the state should have a clear picture of how the enterprise works now, how it needs to work in the future, and what changes are required to get there.

Step 4

Document State Self-Assessment

This step packages the work so it can guide decisions. It captures the maturity results, gives leadership a clear summary, helps prioritize next actions, and turns the assessment into a roadmap for execution.

At a glance

What this step is for

Turn the assessment into a leadership-ready set of decisions, documents, and next actions.

Who is involved

Self-assessment teams, architects, subject matter experts, leadership, and planning staff responsible for the roadmap.

What you produce

A maturity profile, SS-A Executive Summary, draft and final roadmap, updated outcomes, and an execution path for projects.

What your team does

  1. Capture the maturity assessment results in a usable format.
  2. Create an executive summary and roadmap view for leadership.
  3. Review the results with leadership and confirm priorities.
  4. Update outcomes and measures for the priorities selected.
  5. Launch projects and keep measuring progress.
4a

Capture maturity assessment results

Use the method of your choice to document the maturity assessments completed during Step 3.

  • CMS offers an optional proof-of-concept SS-A Tool.
  • Generate a CSV Maturity Profile in the CMS-required format.
  • If not using the optional tool, compile the results in the CMS spreadsheet template and save them as CSV.
  • Import the Maturity Profile CSV into MESH and notify the State Officer that it is ready for review.
4b

Create the SS-A Executive Summary

Use the Executive Summary template and roadmap visual to create a leadership-ready summary.

  • State the goals of the initiative or project.
  • Show prioritized features and functionalities.
  • Document risks and mitigation strategies.
  • Include a visual timeline such as a roadmap or Gantt view.
  • Identify the resources and support needed.
4c

Review the SS-A with leadership

Use the assessment to support prioritization and decision-making.

  • Submit the Executive Summary, Maturity Profile, and draft roadmap for review.
  • Review maturity results with leadership across capability domains.
  • Determine what should be addressed first.
  • Adjust and finalize the roadmap if leadership priorities differ from the initial draft.
4d

Update or define outcomes

Use Outcomes-Based Planning again so the selected priorities have clear measures and ownership.

  • Work with subject matter experts to define or update desired outcomes.
  • Develop or refine the associated metrics.
  • Use outcomes and metrics to assess progress toward success.
4e

Execute projects

Launch the work and keep assessing progress against the outcomes and measures defined earlier in the process.

Decision points after the SS-A

  1. If the goal is achieved, restart the Outcomes-Based Planning process to define the next priority.
  2. If the goal is not yet achieved, continue measuring progress and reassess the outcomes for that priority.
  3. Routinely reassess whether the state’s goals have changed. If they have, restart the process with new priorities.

What You Get

How the pieces come together

The value of MITA 4.0 is not the individual artifacts by themselves. The value comes from how they connect business goals, capability planning, architecture, maturity assessment, and execution.

1

A business challenge and outcome set that explain why the work matters.

2

A capability mapping that links the outcome to the parts of the enterprise that must improve.

3

Architecture views that describe current and future state across business, information, and technology.

4

A maturity profile and SS-A documentation that capture the findings in a consistent format.

5

A roadmap and leadership conversation that turns the assessment into prioritized modernization action.

Best way to use this page

  • Use the main MITA 4.0 overview site when you need the strategic story and product map.
  • Use this page when your team needs to understand the working sequence of the SS-A process.
  • Use the SS-A Tool page when your team is ready to see how the browser-based tool supports documenting the assessment.
  • Use the outputs from each step as the starting inputs for the next step rather than treating them as standalone documents.
